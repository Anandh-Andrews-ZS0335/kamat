LAST MILE — DEMO NARRATION
Read at a normal pace, about 150 words a minute. Pause where marked — the pauses are
where the screen does the work. ≈ 820 spoken words, ≈ 5 minutes 30 seconds.

Timecodes match docs/demo/demo-shot-list.md.


[0:00 — title card]

Every bank already knows who is likely to default. Almost none of them know what to do
about it on a Tuesday morning.

[pause]


[0:08 — Today, empty]

This is Last Mile. It sits on top of a bank's existing risk model and answers the only
question a collections manager actually has: who do I contact today, what do I offer, and
why.

Nothing here is a mock-up. This is running on a server, against a bank API, on a real
business date.

[pause]


[0:28 — confirm today's team]

It starts with the one constraint that is always true — how many people are on the floor,
and for how long. I'm putting one collector on a short shift today.

That number is not decoration. It becomes a hard constraint in the optimiser, and you'll see
it come back in a moment.

[pause]


[0:50 — start the run]

Now the run. Eight agents, in a fixed order.

They pull six feeds from the bank, run thirteen quality checks, and move every name and phone
number into a vault the models never see. They recalibrate the bank's own risk score onto a
common scale — this bank sends letter grades, another one we support sends a 180-day
probability, and the engine doesn't care which.

[pause]

Then the part that matters. A causal model estimates, for every account and every possible
action, how much that action would change the chance of the customer catching up. Not how
risky they are — how much difference a phone call would make. Those are different questions,
and only the second one tells you where to spend an hour.

[pause]

Every step is recorded: which agent ran, which tool it called, how long it took. An agent can
only call the tools it owns.

[pause]


[1:45 — team queues]

And here's that short shift coming back. This isn't one long list to work down. It's one queue
per person, each packed to fit the hours that person is actually working. The collector I cut
to a half day has a shorter queue — not a truncated one, a re-planned one.

[pause]


[2:07 — one account]

Let me open a single account, because this is where a recommendation either holds up or falls
apart.

The system recommends one specific action, and tells me what it's worth in money, not in
probability.

[pause]

Every figure on this screen carries its source. Hover any of them and it says which field, in
which feed, from which run.

[pause]

This is the script the collector will actually read. And this is every option considered for
this account — including the ones that were blocked, and why. This customer isn't eligible for
a payment plan yet, so that action doesn't exist for them, whatever it might have been worth.

[pause]

Then the question a manager always asks. Why this account, and not another one?

It answers by re-solving the whole day with this account forced out, and showing what the plan
would have been worth without it. A real counterfactual — not a sentence written afterwards to
sound convincing.

[pause]


[3:17 — sort vs optimised]

Here's the same day planned three ways: sorted by risk, sorted by expected loss, and optimised.
The gap on screen is the difference between a sorted list and actual prescriptive work. The
solver will skip an expensive high-risk account in favour of two cheaper ones, because that
recovers more from the same hours.

[pause]


[3:42 — approve and release]

Nothing so far has contacted anybody.

I approve the ones I'm happy with. Bulk approval deliberately leaves the escalated ones behind
— hardship referrals are never approved in a batch, because that's a judgement about someone's
circumstances.

Then I release. That's the first moment anything leaves this system.

[pause]


[4:07 — the collector's queue]

Same system, signed in as one of the collectors.

She sees the accounts assigned to her, in the order the optimiser chose, and she can re-order
them — she's the one on the phone, and she knows things the model doesn't.

[pause]

What she does not see is any of the money. No expected value, no uplift, no risk grade, no
segment label. That's deliberate. Telling somebody the person they're about to ring is a
low-value lost cause changes how that conversation goes. Those fields aren't hidden on this
page — they're never sent to it.

[pause]

After the call she records what happened. A promise to pay, with the date. That goes back into
tomorrow's plan: contact history, how long calls really take, and any hard stop like a wrong
number.

[pause]


[4:55 — governance]

Every call to a language model is stored — the prompt, the reply, and whether the validator
accepted it. The model is used in exactly two places in this system: writing the wording of a
reason, and reading an unfamiliar bank's column names when we onboard one. It never produces a
number and never chooses an action.

Everything else — the uplift model, the value, the solver, the simulation, the rules — is
deterministic code. And every decision sits in a hash-chained audit that verifies on read.

[pause]


[5:20 — close]

A prediction became a decision, a person approved it, and what happened next comes back to
train the next one.

That's the last mile.

[end]

NOTES FOR THE RECORDING

Do not read any money figure aloud. The narration refers to what's on screen generically, so
it stays true whatever the run produces on the day.

The two longest pauses are at 1:35 (the run finishing) and 2:55 (the counterfactual
re-solving). If your recording runs faster than the screen, stretch those two first.

One line to land cleanly, because it is the judging criterion: "The model is used in exactly
two places in this system." Slow down there.

For a 3-minute cut, drop these whole blocks: 0:00, the third paragraph of 0:50 ("Every step is
recorded…"), 3:17, and 4:55. The account walkthrough at 2:07 is the one section that must
survive — it is the only place the system proves it is prescriptive rather than a sorted list
with narration.
