LAST MILE — DEMO NARRATION
Read at a normal pace, roughly 145 words a minute. Pause where marked; the pauses are
where the screen does the work. Total ≈ 4 minutes 40 seconds of speech.

Timecodes match docs/demo/demo-shot-list.md.


[0:00 — title card]

Every bank already knows who is likely to default. Almost none of them know what to do
about it on a Tuesday morning.

[pause]


[0:08 — Today, empty]

This is Last Mile. It sits on top of a bank's existing risk model and answers the only
question a collections manager actually has: who do I contact today, what do I offer, and
why.

Nothing here is a mock-up. This is running on a server, against a bank API, on a real
business date.

[pause]


[0:25 — confirm today's team]

It starts with the one constraint that is always true — how many people are on the floor,
and for how long. I'm putting one collector on a short shift today.

That number is not decoration. It becomes a hard constraint in the optimiser, and you will
see it come back in a moment.

[pause]


[0:48 — start the run]

Now the run. Eight agents, in a fixed order.

They pull six feeds from the bank, run thirteen quality checks, and move every name and
phone number into a vault that the models never see. They recalibrate the bank's own risk
score onto a common scale — this bank sends letter grades, another one we support sends a
180-day probability, and the engine does not care which.

Then the part that matters. A causal model estimates, for every account and every possible
action, how much that action would change the chance of the customer catching up. Not how
risky they are — how much difference a phone call would make. Those are different questions,
and only the second one tells you where to spend an hour.

The whole thing takes about twenty seconds.

[pause]

Every step you just watched is recorded — which agent ran, which tool it called, how long it
took. An agent can only call the tools it owns; if it reaches for another one, it is refused
and the refusal is logged.

[pause]


[1:30 — team queues]

And here is that short shift coming back. This isn't one long list to work down. It is one
queue per person, each one packed to fit the hours that person is actually working. The
collector I cut to a half day has a shorter queue — not a truncated one, a re-planned one.

[pause]


[1:58 — one account]

Let me open a single account, because this is where a recommendation either holds up or
falls apart.

The system is recommending one specific action, and it is telling me what that is worth in
pounds and pence, not in probability.

Every figure on this screen carries its source. Hover any of them and it tells you which
field in which feed it came from, and which run produced it. Nothing here is a number
somebody's model made up.

[pause]

This is the script the collector will actually read. And this is every option that was
considered for this account — including the ones that were blocked, and why. This customer
isn't eligible for a payment plan yet, so that action doesn't exist for them, whatever it
might have been worth.

[pause]

And then the question a manager always asks. Why this account, and not another one?

The system answers it by re-solving the whole day with this account forced out, and telling
you what the plan would have been worth without it. That is a real counterfactual, not a
sentence generated after the fact to sound convincing.

[pause]


[3:00 — sort vs optimised]

Here is the same day planned three ways — sorted by risk, sorted by expected loss, and
optimised. The gap on screen is the difference between a sorted list and actual prescriptive
work. The solver will skip an expensive high-risk account in favour of two cheaper ones,
because that is what recovers more money from the same hours.

[pause]


[3:25 — approve and release]

Nothing you have seen so far has contacted anybody.

I approve the ones I'm happy with. Bulk approval deliberately leaves the escalated ones
behind — hardship referrals are never approved in a batch, because that is a judgement about
someone's circumstances.

And then I release. That is the first moment anything leaves this system.

[pause]


[3:45 — the collector's queue]

This is the same system, signed in as one of the collectors.

She sees the accounts assigned to her, in the order the optimiser chose, and she can re-order
them — she is the one on the phone and she knows things the model doesn't.

What she does not see is any of the money. No expected value, no uplift, no risk grade, and
no segment label. That is deliberate. Telling somebody that the person they are about to ring
is a low-value lost cause changes how that conversation goes. Those fields are not hidden on
this page — they are never sent to it.

[pause]

After the call, she records what happened. A promise to pay, with the date. That goes back
into tomorrow's plan: contact history, how long calls really take, and any hard stop like a
wrong number. Corrections are appended, never overwritten, so the note she made at the time
survives.

[pause]


[4:20 — governance]

Every call to a language model is stored — the prompt, the reply, and whether the validator
accepted it. The model is used in exactly two places in this system: writing the wording of a
reason, and reading an unfamiliar bank's column names when we onboard one. It never produces
a number, never chooses an action, and never sees customer data.

Everything else — the uplift model, the value, the solver, the simulation, the rules — is
deterministic code. Same inputs, same outputs, every time. And every decision is in a
hash-chained audit that verifies on read.

[pause]


[4:35 — close]

A prediction became a decision, a person approved it, and what happened next comes back to
train the next one.

That is the last mile.

[end]

NOTES FOR THE RECORDING

• Do not read any dollar figure aloud. The narration refers to what is on screen
  generically, so it stays true whatever the run produces on the day.

• The two longest pauses are at 1:12 (the run finishing) and 2:35 (the counterfactual
  re-solving). If your recording runs faster than the screen, stretch those two.

• If you cut to three minutes: drop the paragraphs at 0:00, the second half of 0:48
  ("Every step you just watched…"), all of 3:00, and all of 4:20. The account walkthrough
  at 1:58 is the one section that must survive.

• One line worth landing cleanly, because it is the judging criterion: "The model is used in
  exactly two places in this system." Slow down there.
