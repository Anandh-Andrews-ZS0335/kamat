01

What we actually predict

Three different predictions get confused with each other in collections. Only the third one is ours, and it is the one the whole system is built around.

Risk of default — the bank's, not ours

Probability the account defaults. The bank's own model produces it and sends it in the scores feed. We never retrain it. Riverbend sends grades A–E; Harbor sends a 180-day probability. We calibrate whatever arrives onto one scale (pd_12m, pd_h).

Used for: how much money is at stake, not who to call.

Base cure probability — ours

P(the account catches up within 30 days if we do nothing). Learnt from history where the action recorded was NONE.

Used for: separating people who will recover anyway from people who need help.

Uplift — ours, and the real answer

For each account and each of SMS, CALL, PLAN and HARDSHIP: P(cure | we do this) − P(cure | we do nothing), with a low–high interval from six bagged models.

Used for: choosing the action. This is the prediction the mentor's question is asking about.

Why uplift and not "who is most likely to pay". A ranking by risk sends collectors to the people most likely to default, many of whom will default whatever we do. A ranking by likelihood-to-pay sends them to people who would have paid anyway. Uplift ranks by difference the call makes, which is the only one of the three that describes the value of a collector's hour.

Five fields are banned from the model on purpose

curr_bal, dpd, status, cured_30d and amt_paid_30d describe the account after the decision window. A request for any of them raises an error rather than silently training a model that scores 0.95 and is worthless. Any new field proposed below has to survive the same test.

04

Three fields arrive and are thrown away

The cheapest improvement available. No new bank integration, no new consent — the data is already crossing the wire.

language

What we do now: carry it to the vault, then ignore it.

What it should do: choose the script language and, plausibly, the channel — an SMS in the wrong language is a wasted contact that still counts against the daily cap.

Effort: small. It is a template-selection key, not a model feature.

email_consent

What we do now: join it into the portfolio and never read it.

What it should do: gate an EMAIL action, which does not exist yet. Email costs near-zero minutes, so adding it widens what the optimiser can do with a fixed team.

Effort: medium — a new action needs a cost, an uplift arm and history to learn from.

last_rpc_date

What we do now: ignore it, and recompute recency from the collections queue.

What it should do: capture contact that happened outside the collections queue. This is exactly the blind spot behind our 20 over-cap customer-days — we cannot see the bank's own contacts, so we cannot count them.

Effort: small, and it closes a live compliance gap.

The collection agent can now be the source

Since this page was written, the collection agent console has been built: an agent signs in, sees only the queue assigned to them, and may re-order their own work before release. What it does not yet have is a way to record what happened on each attempt — so every contact-and-interaction row in the table above still has to come from the bank rather than from the person who made the call. Adding outcome capture on that console is the cheapest route to most of that group.

Where agents do the gathering — answering the "can agents enrich?" question directly

Already built: the Onboarding Agent reads a bank's OpenAPI description, profiles its columns as value shapes rather than values, proposes the mapping, and an admin approves it. Adding a new feed is a review, not a code change. It mapped Harbor's 54 fields correctly on the first run.

The natural next agent: an Enrichment Agent that turns unstructured text into a small number of structured, dated flags — promise_made, promise_date, hardship_mentioned, dispute_raised, distress_signal. The LLM reads text and emits flags; it never emits a probability, a dollar figure or a decision. Those flags then enter the uplift model as ordinary point-in-time features and are subject to the same leakage guard as every other field.

The discipline that has to travel with it: a flag extracted from a call on day 40 may not appear on a training row dated day 12. Text enrichment is the easiest place in this whole system to build a model that looks excellent and is cheating.

07

What more data will not fix

The review asks whether broader data improves the prediction. Mostly yes — but a causal model has a different bottleneck from a risk model, and it is worth being straight about it.

Uplift is limited by variation in treatment, not by feature count

The model learns from differences between what happened to contacted and uncontacted accounts. If the bank has always called the same kind of customer, no number of new columns will separate the effect of the call from the kind of person who gets called. Five per cent randomisation buys more accuracy here than fifty new fields.

Feedback moves at 30 days, whatever we add

The outcome is "cured within 30 days", so a change made today is measurable in a month. Richer inputs do not speed that up. Shorter-horizon secondary outcomes — a payment within 7 days, a kept promise — would, and they are worth adding for that reason alone.

Every new field widens the compliance surface

Income, bureau data and call recordings each carry their own purpose limits and consent requirements. The architecture holds up — personal data stays in the vault, the model works on tokens, the LLM sees shapes — but adding a field is a permission question before it is an engineering question.

More signal makes leakage easier, not harder

The five banned fields exist because the obvious ones are easy to spot. A promise flag extracted from a call that happened after the decision date is not obvious, and it will produce a beautiful, useless model. Point-in-time discipline has to grow with the data, which is why the leakage guard raises an error instead of logging a warning.
