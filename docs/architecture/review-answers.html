The six questions

Q1

What prediction does our solution provide?

For each delinquent account and each available action, we predict P(cures within 30 days | we take this action) − P(cures within 30 days | we do nothing), with a confidence range. That difference is called uplift.

  • We do not predict default risk. The bank's own model does that and sends it to us. We calibrate it to a common scale and use it to size the money at stake, never to choose who to call.
  • Output per account: the recommended action, its uplift and range, its expected value in dollars, a segment, a rank, an assigned collector, a queue position, and a sentence saying why.
  • Why not "who is most likely to pay": that ranking sends collectors to people who would have paid anyway. Uplift ranks by the difference the call makes, which is what a collector hour is actually worth.

Q2

What decision is taken by AI / LLM, and what is the role of the agent?

The LLM takes no decision at all. The agents take no decision either — they run stages and call tools. The decisions are made by rules, by arithmetic, by a model, by a solver, and by a person.

  • What the LLM does — two jobs, both language. It drafts the wording of the reason shown to the manager, from segment and action only, with figures filled in afterwards from stamped facts and checked by a validator. And it proposes how a new bank's columns map to ours, which is then verified in code and approved by an admin.
  • What the LLM never does. It never produces a number, never picks an action, never sees a customer's name, balance or phone number.
  • What an agent is here. A stage with a fixed job and a fixed set of tools it is allowed to call. The registry enforces that — the Feature Agent cannot call the optimiser even if it tried. Agents give us ordering, traceability and isolation, not judgement.
  • Who decides the action. The uplift model ranks, the solver allocates under constraints, and the manager approves. Three different mechanisms, each auditable on its own.

Q3

Where do we use AI agents and where do we use rule-based logic?

The rule we applied: if policy or law defines the answer, it is a rule. If it is arithmetic, it is arithmetic. If it needs a pattern across many weak signals, it is a model. If it is a constrained allocation, it is a solver. The LLM gets only what is genuinely about language.

  • Rules: consent, do-not-call, deceased, bankruptcy, litigation, fraud hold, hardship eligibility, days-past-due thresholds, contact caps. Written as structured conditions in YAML that an admin can read and change without retraining anything.
  • Rules run twice. Once before scoring, to say what is allowed. Once after optimisation, as an independent re-check of the finished plan. A rule that only ran first would not catch a plan that broke it afterwards.
  • Model: only the uplift estimate. It is the one question no rule can answer — no policy can tell you who is persuadable.
  • Solver: which actions fit today's capacity, and who works what. A solver can prove its answer is feasible; a language model would guess.

Q4

Can we expand the data points used?

Yes, and the first three cost nothing: we already receive language, email_consent and last_rpc_date and never read them. Beyond that, the biggest single gap is a per-attempt call log.

  • Found while preparing this answer: three fields cross the wire every morning and are discarded. last_rpc_date matters most — it would capture contact made outside the collections queue, which is exactly the blind spot behind the cap breaches we measured.
  • Structured data worth asking a real bank for: payment and transaction history, salary credit dates, other balances at the same bank, failed direct debits, per-attempt call logs with real durations, SMS delivery events, inbound contact, and one combined view of all contact rather than just ours.
  • Unstructured data: call recordings and transcripts, collector free-text notes, complaint correspondence. An LLM turns these into a few dated flags — promise made, hardship mentioned, dispute raised — which then enter the model as ordinary features under the same leakage rules.
  • Agents already do the gathering. The Onboarding Agent reads a bank's API, profiles its columns as value shapes rather than values, and proposes the mapping for an admin to approve. It mapped a second bank's 54 fields correctly on the first attempt. Adding a new feed is a review, not a code change.

Q5

Can broader data and AI analysis improve the prediction?

Partly — and this is the one place we want to push back on the premise. A causal model has a different bottleneck from a risk model. Uplift is limited by variation in treatment, not by feature count.

  • Where more data genuinely helps: a payment trajectory beats three summary counts; real call durations remove a fixed assumption that currently caps how accurately we can plan a shift; promise and distress flags from text capture intent that structured fields cannot.
  • Where it does not help: if the bank has always called the same kind of customer, no number of new columns can separate the effect of the call from the kind of person who receives one. That is a property of the data-generating process, not of the model.
  • What actually unlocks it: leaving a small random sample of eligible accounts alone. Five per cent randomisation buys more accuracy here than fifty new fields, and it is the only thing that turns "we observed this" into "we caused this".
  • And a warning: richer signals make leakage easier, not harder. A promise flag from a call that happened after the decision date produces a beautiful, useless model. Our leakage guard raises an error rather than logging a warning, and any new field has to pass it.

Q6

Where should humans be in the decision?

A person approves every single action before it reaches a customer. Nothing is auto-sent. The role model the review asked for is now built and enforced on every request.

  • Automated: pulling data, checking quality, calibrating risk, estimating uplift, computing value, optimising, packing queues, and applying every policy rule. All deterministic and all reproducible.
  • Always human: releasing actions to the bank, and anything escalated — hardship referrals, large exposures, and cases where the uplift range is too wide to trust. Saving configuration and onboarding a bank are admin-only.
  • Three roles, enforced in the API: admin does everything including onboarding, configuration and creating any account; collections manager runs the day and adds collection agents to their own floor, but cannot open configuration or onboarding; collection agent sees only the queue assigned to them and may re-order their own work before release. A read-only guest role existed and was withdrawn, because it could read configuration that a manager cannot — an inverted permission is worse than a missing one.
  • Signing in resolves per role. Each role lands on a page it can open, and a refused page answers with a page explaining which role the account has, rather than a JSON error.
  • The two things that make it real rather than cosmetic. A collector's scope comes from the signed-in session, so a collector id in the URL is ignored. And the roles a person may hand out are capped by their own — a manager who posts role: "manager" by hand gets a 403, not a new peer.
  • Still open: a collector can work and re-order their queue but cannot yet record what happened on each attempt, so the same-day half of the feedback loop is not closed. See KPI answer Q5.
